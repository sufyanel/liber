5-Axis CNC Machining: When to Make the Leap

MC
Mike Chen · Senior Manufacturing Engineer · June 15, 2026 · 8 min read

Five-axis machining is no longer reserved for aerospace and medical device manufacturers. As 5-axis machines become more accessible and control software more intuitive, many mid-size job shops and contract manufacturers are evaluating the transition. But is it right for your operation?

The Case for 5-Axis

The primary advantage of 5-axis machining is the ability to machine complex geometries in a single setup. This eliminates multiple fixturing operations, reduces handling errors, and compresses lead times. For parts with compound angles, deep cavities, or undercuts, 5-axis isn't just better — it's the only practical option.

Breaking Down the Economics

Any capital equipment decision comes down to ROI. For 5-axis machining, the key variables are:

  • Setup reduction: A single 5-axis setup vs. 3–5 setups on a 3-axis machine can cut total cycle time by 40–60%
  • Fixture savings: Eliminating special fixturing for compound angles can save $500–$2,000 per part number
  • Quality improvement: Fewer setups means fewer opportunities for datum errors — scrap rates typically drop 15–25%
  • Machine premium: A 5-axis machining center costs 60–100% more than an equivalent 3-axis machine

When to Stay with 3-Axis

Not every part benefits from 5-axis. If your work is predominantly 2.5D prismatic parts (pockets, drilled holes, simple contours), a well-maintained 3-axis machine with a trunnion table or indexer will handle the work at a lower cost-per-part. The thumb rule: if more than 20% of your part numbers require compound-angle machining or deep-cavity work, 5-axis is worth the investment.

Case Study: 35% Cost Reduction on Pump Housing

One of our clients — a Midwest pump manufacturer — was producing a complex housing casting across four separate 3-axis setups. Total cycle time: 4.2 hours. Scrap rate: 12% (primarily from datum stackup errors). We moved the part to a Mazak 5-axis HMC with a single setup. Cycle time dropped to 2.1 hours. Scrap rate fell to under 2%. Annual savings: $187,000 on a run of 2,400 parts.

"We were skeptical about 5-axis for what seemed like a simple housing. But the data didn't lie — the breakeven was 18 months on a $450,000 machine investment." — Plant Manager, Midwest Pump Co.

Getting Started: A Practical Framework

If you're considering the transition, here's a structured approach:

  1. Audit your part portfolio — Classify each active part number by geometry complexity, number of setups, and scrap rate
  2. Run a 5-axis simulation — Many CAM packages (Mastercam, Siemens NX) can simulate 5-axis toolpaths on your existing 3D models
  3. Calculate the "pain score" — (Setups × handling time) + (scrap rate × part value) = savings opportunity per part
  4. Test with a machine shop partner — Run a sample batch on our 5-axis machines before committing to capital
